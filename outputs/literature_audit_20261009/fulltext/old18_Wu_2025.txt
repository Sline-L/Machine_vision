                               Automated Optical Inspection (AOI) technology is crucial for industrial defect detection but struggles
                               with shadows and surface reflectivity, resulting in false positives and missed detections, especially
                               on non-planar parts. To address these issues, a novel defect detection technique based on deep
                               learning and photometric stereo vision was proposed, along with the creation of the Metal Surface
                               Defect Dataset (MSDD). The proposed Stroboscopic Illuminant Image Acquisition (SIIA) method uses
                               a specially arranged illuminant setup and a Taylor Series Channel Mixer (TSCM) to blend multi-angle
                               illumination images into pseudo-color images. This approach enables end-to-end defect detection
                               using universal object detectors. The method involves mapping color space transformations to spatial
                               domain transformations and utilizing hue randomization for data augmentation. Four object detection
                               methods (FCOS, YOLOv5, YOLOv8, and RT-DETR) were validated on the MSDD, achieving an mAP of
                               86.1%, surpassing traditional methods. The MSDD includes 138,585 single-channel images and 9,239
                               mixed images, covering eight defect types. This dataset is essential for automated visual inspection of
                               metal surfaces and is freely accessible for research purposes.



                               Background & Summary
                               Surface defect detection is essential in industrial production, ensuring the aesthetic quality and mechanical
                               integrity of metal parts. Common defects such as slag inclusions, dents, cracks, insufficient casting, and pitting
                               can significantly compromise product performance and pose serious safety risks. As reported by the NHTSA’s
                               2022 Annual Recalls Report, over 30.8 million vehicles were recalled in the United States due to quality issues,
                               resulting in economic losses exceeding trillions of dollars for the automotive industry1.
                                   Automated Optical Inspection (AOI)2 technology has become increasingly important in detecting surface
                               defects in metal products, offering several advantages over manual inspection, such as high efficiency, labor
                               cost savings, and reduced susceptibility to subjective interference. The fundamental principle of AOI systems
                               lies in utilizing the optical properties of metal surfaces. Under specific optical imaging conditions, defects
                               exhibit distinct image features that differ from the background. 2D imaging systems and 3D imaging systems are
                               subsequently employed to identify and localize defects or anomalies3.
                               2D imaging systems.            Previous research in this field has predominantly focused on defect detection using
                               2D imaging systems4–8, which heavily rely on the Lambertian reflection model9. When light interacts with a
                               metal surface, it produces Lambertian, directional, and specular reflections. Various lighting techniques such as
                               bright-field, dark-field, diffuse reflection, and backlighting10 are applied, as illustrated in Fig. 1(a-c). From these
                               reflections, 2D images are captured, and defects are identified and located based on the contrast in brightness lev-
                               els between the defects and the background. Several surface defect detection datasets have been established based
                               on 2D imaging technologies, with the most commonly used ones being the Northeastern University Surface
                               Defect Database (NEU-DET)11 and the GC10-DET. These datasets have been utilized for surface defect detection
                               using general-purpose object detection models12–14.
                                   While these studies have made some progress in enhancing the efficiency and accuracy of the models, they
                               are restricted to 2D static images and lack critical advancements in optical system design, causing two main

                                 Contributions of this research.           To address the existing challenges in metal surface defect detection, par-
                                 ticularly the sensitivity to lighting angles and the impact of low reflectivity on complex surfaces, this paper pre-
                                 sents several notable contributions. These contributions focus on innovative imaging techniques, dataset creation,
                                 and comprehensive method validation. The major contributions are as follows:

                                 (a) Development of an imaging method for non-planar parts with complex structures: This study establishes a
                                     real-time 3D imaging defect detection framework based on photometric stereo and deep learning. A Stro-
                                     boscopic Illuminant Image Acquisition (SIIA) method is proposed for capturing images from light sources
                                     at all surrounding angles to address the issue of complex surface structures of parts, as shown in Fig. 1(e).
                                     By mapping color space transformations to spatial domain transformations, end-to-end optimization of
                                     the imaging system and surface defect detectors is achieved, effectively addressing sensitivity to illuminant
                                     angles and the difficulties in designing complex optical systems.
                                 (b) Collecting and constructing of the Metal Surface Defect Detection dataset: The MSDD aluminum casting
                                     surface defect detection dataset is created, consisting of 9,332 annotated images covering eight defect types,
                                     valuable for metal part surface defect detection research. It is demonstrated that metal castings produced
                                     by the gravity casting process exhibit consistent defect patterns, making this dataset highly valuable for
                                     research in metal part surface defect detection technology.

                                    These contributions collectively advance the field by addressing key limitations in current metal surface
                                 defect detection techniques, fostering the development of more accurate, efficient, and scalable solutions for
                                 industrial use.

                             Optical imaging.           We propose the SIIM based on photometric stereo. This method uses m CCD cameras
                             and n illuminants arranged circumferentially around the detection object (Fig. 2a). While the schematic diagram
                             simplifies the arrangement into a ring for clarity, in actual application, the cameras and illuminants are positioned
                             at different heights to illuminate from various vertical and horizontal angles, capturing richer surface detail infor-
                             mation. A programmable logic controller (PLC) alternates and synchronizes the strobing of these illuminants
                             with the cameras, obtaining images illuminated from different angles and perspectives, as shown in Fig. 2(a).
                             Each camera works in conjunction with the illuminants at 30 frames per second (FPS), allowing the entire image
                             acquisition process to be completed within 0.96 seconds, which is sufficient to meet the production cycle require-
                             ments of 300 pieces per hour on a typical aluminum alloy casting line.

                             Image mixing. This paper proposes a Taylor Series Channel Mixer (TSCM) that combines images from
                             the three channels into an RGB pseudo-color image as shown in Fig. 2(c). The n illuminants are arranged in a
                             cyclic symmetric manner, with an angle T between adjacent illuminants (Fig. 2a). First, each illuminant flashes
                             sequentially, capturing n images with different illumination angles, denoted as 0, 1, 2, …, n-1, and stacking them
                             into Tensor A of shape w × h × n. Next, as shown in Fig. 2(b), all channels are cyclically shifted by one position
                             along the stacking direction to obtain Tensor B, and the shifting operation continues to obtain Tensor C. The
                             three tensors are respectively input into the TSCM with shared parameters, each outputting a tensor of w × h × 1.,
                             serving as the Red, Green, and Blue channels of the mixed image to form an RGB pseudo-color image. Under
                             these conditions, in the local region, for any point (x, y) on the metal surface, the positional relationship of the
                             nearest three illuminants Lk, Lk+1, and Lk+2 is as shown in Fig. 2(d), staggered in the height direction (z-axis). By
                             optimizing the mixing parameters, the influence of illuminants farther away, such as Lk–1, and Lk+3, etc., whose
                             angular deviation is greater than the reflective lobe range of the metal surface, on the color values at (x, y) is min-
                             imized and can thus be ignored.
                                 The structure of the channel mixer, as illustrated in Fig. 3, includes a nonlinear activation layer and a linear
                             activation layer. This design draws inspiration from the Kolmogorov-Arnold Network (KAN). According to the
                             Kolmogorov-Arnold theorem, if f is a multivariate continuous function defined on a bounded domain, then f
                             can be expressed as a finite composition of continuous functions of a single variable and the binary operation
                             of addition. Unlike the classical KAN, which utilizes B-Splines, the channel mixer’s nonlinear layer employs a
                             fourth-order Taylor series expansion, followed by summation in the linear activation layer.

                                 Regarding the overall layout, the relationship between these three tensors approximates the new images
                             obtained by sequentially rotating the entire illuminant array by an angle T and illuminating them, while being
                             mixed through the same channel mixer. Under this conditions, the three channels of the mixed image in the
                             HSV color space are equivalent to a hue transformation rotated by 120 degrees. This method allows us to simu-
                             late approximately any direction of illumination conditions without moving the physical illuminants, eliminat-
                             ing the problem of small defects being extremely sensitive to the lighting angle.
                                 The nonlinear layer employs a fourth-order Taylor series expansion. Because the image pixel values are nor-
                             malized to the range of 0–1, this fourth-order Taylor expansion can approximate any ideal mixing function fmix
                             with high accuracy within this range. Additionally, compared to the MLP structure in classic CNN networks,
                             the TSCM consists of only two layers, which helps avoid issues such as gradient vanishing and exploding during
                             backpropagation, thereby leading to more stable network convergence.
                                 The channel mixer is parameter-optimized using a Vector Quantized Variational Autoencoder (VQ-VAE)23,
                             as shown in Fig. 4. The w × h × 3. mixed image is input into the autoencoder, outputting a w ×h × n. recon-
                             structed image, and calculating the loss with the original w × h × n. image. The loss function employs Mean
                             Squared Error Loss (MSE Loss), Structural Similarity Loss (SSIM Loss), Vector Quantizer Loss (Vector
                             Quantizer Loss), and Commitment Loss (Commitment Loss). The MSE Loss minimizes the information loss

                             structed image. The Vector Quantizer Loss Lvq makes the codebook vector e approximate the latent variable z
                             output by the encoder, while the Commitment Loss Lcommit makes the latent variable z output by the encoder
                             approximate the codebook vector e. They can be expressed as:

                             Feature extraction (Optional).             This paper proposes a Phase-Invariant Feature Module (PIFM) to extract
                             shallow feature information from images that is invariant to hue transformations, thereby eliminating the sensi-
                             tivity of defect detection models to lighting angles. Notably, this step is optional. Unlike VQ-VAE, this method
                             reconstructs images directly using the latent variable z to reduce information loss, while using e as the learning
                             target for PIFM, as shown in Fig. 4(c). Quantization loss plays a crucial role in the autoencoder. By constructing
                             a discrete and stable latent space, it helps the model learn features that are insensitive to subtle changes in lighting
                             in the input image. By mapping continuous latent representations into a finite, predefined codebook space, the
                             discrete vectors of the vectorized representation reduce the network’s sensitivity to changes in lighting angles,
                             encouraging the network to learn consistent, abstract, and conceptual information under different lighting con-
                             ditions. This modular design allows the PIFM module to be easily integrated into other object detection networks.
                             The encoder is designed as an independent feature extraction module responsible for extracting features from the
                             image that are invariant to lighting angles, and can directly replace the P1 and P2 layers of many object detection
                             networks, training the network using conventional methods.

                             Data preprocessing. The dataset collected 1,507 metal parts with defects. Images were captured multiple
                             times under different lighting conditions by six cameras, resulting in an initially large dataset. This method uti-
                             lizes high-resolution cameras (up to 2560 × 1920 pixels) to detect defects as small as 0.5 mm. These images enable
                             the model to effectively identify tiny defect features. To manage large image sizes, a sliding window technique
                             divides images into 640 × 640 patches, with a 15% overlap to ensure complete defect detection. All images were
                             slightly distorted, scaled, and sheared to obscure the true dimensions and shapes of the products. Additionally,
                             all logos and identifying marks were removed to prevent potential commercial disputes. Experimental results
                             indicate that these modifications did not significantly affect defect detection performance.

                             Defect detection.          The images collected in this study can be utilized for training and detection in general
                             object detection models by simply inputting the TSCM-mixed RGB pseudo-color images into these networks,
                             similar to how other defect detection datasets are used. To incorporate the PIFM and enable end-to-end optimi-
                             zation, the P1-P3 stages of the backbones in conventional defect detection models can be replaced with the PIFM,
                             as illustrated in Fig. 5a. This modification is independent of the object detection model architecture, whether
                             it is anchor-free, anchor-based, or ViT-based. Accordingly, we have tested this approach on FCOS24 (Fig. 5b),
                             YOLOv525, YOLOv826 (Fig. 5c), and RT-DETR27 (Fig. 5d). The Ultralytics26 framework was utilized to facilitate
                             the rapid construction of object detection networks, although it is not essential to our method.
                                  The fully modular design endows this network with robust generalization capabilities, enabling the replace-
                             ment of the object detector with any standard object detection network architecture. The encoder is responsible
                             for extracting features from the image that are invariant to lighting angles, and can directly replace the P1-P3
                             layers of many object detection networks, training the network using conventional methods. By pre-training the
                             model’s feature extraction layers in this way, convergence speed and detection accuracy can be improved. The
                             hue transformation is entirely implemented on tensors to maintain gradient flow, making the proposed method
                             end-to-end optimizable, allowing fine-tuning of the channel mixer and feature extractor even in the final stages
                             of defect detection. Compared to static mixing methods, this approach not only addresses the sensitivity to illu-
                             minant angles but also provides stronger feature representation capabilities.

                             Technical Validation
                             The experiments were conducted on a computer with an Intel Xeon(R) Silver 4110 CPU @ 2.10 GHz processor,
                             32 GB memory, 24 GB NVIDIA GeForce 3090 GPU, and Windows 10 operating system. The network was built
                             using the Pytorch 1.10.1 framework. The dataset used was the MSDD dataset, split into training and testing sets
                             in a 4:1 ratio.
                                 To verify the performance improvement of the proposed method, ablation experiments were conducted
                             using various improved methods to evaluate their impact on the model’s performance. As mentioned earlier, the
                             improvements mainly include:

                             (a) Linear channel mixer;
                             (b) Adding a nonlinear expression branch utilizing CNN and ReLU to give the channel mixer nonlinear
                                 expression capability;
                             (c) Using TSCM;
                             (d) Using a Vector Quantization layer to construct a discrete latent space, and applying random hue phase shift
                                 transformations to make the defect detection network insensitive to subtle changes in illuminant direction,
                                 and improving the robustness of the detection model.

                                The ablation experiments mainly compared the reconstruction image errors of different mixing methods
                             on the test set, including Mean Squared Error (MSE), Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), Structural Similarity
                             Index (SSIM), and mean Average Precision (mAP) at IoU threshold 0.50 (mAP50) for defect detection.
                                The results of the ablation experiments are shown in Table 3. The results indicate that the TSCM improved
                             the PSNR by 6.02 to 6.03. The vector quantization layer had an insignificant impact on reconstruction quality
                             but increased the mean Average Precision for defect detection by 1.6%. The former shows blurred details and
                             some information loss, while the latter produces reconstructed images almost indistinguishable from the orig-
                             inal images to the naked eye.
                                To further verify the effectiveness of this method, we compared it with state-of-the-art general object
                             detection networks, including FCOS, YOLOv5, YOLOv8, and RT-DETR. The baseline version used a static
                             image mixing method, with mixing parameters optimized by the autoencoder; the improved version used an
                             end-to-end optimized image mixing method with gradient-based random phase shift transformations for data
                             augmentation. Additionally, as a control, the model input channels were modified to n, and the n-channel orig-

                                 The complex geometric shapes of the materials cause 100% of the samples to be affected by shadows and
                             occlusions, significantly impacting detection performance. Additionally, low reflectivity caused by stains is chal-
                             lenging to quantify since stains, as non-defects, are not annotated. However, as aluminum alloy casting blanks
                             are often stored outdoors, they are exposed to environmental factors such as rain and hurricanes, leading to
                             batch occurrences of stains, which contributes to a significant number of false detections in real-world settings.
                                 To address these challenges, the proposed method integrates photometric stereo-based strobe imaging and
                             deep learning to enhance metal surface defect detection. Its effectiveness is validated using the high-quality
                             MSDD dataset. As shown in Table 5, compared to NEU-DET and GC10-DET, the MSDD dataset includes not
                             only a larger collection of grayscale images but also RGB pseudo-color images with depth information, adding
                             an extra layer of detail for more accurate detection. This makes the MSDD dataset an essential tool for address-
                             ing surface defect detection on non-planar materials, a critical challenge in industrial applications.
                                 The key elements of the proposed method to overcome these challenges are outlined below:

                             (a) Imaging and illumination. The Stroboscopic Illuminant Image Acquisition (SIIA) method captures im-
                                 ages from multiple angles to address complex surface geometries, combining them into RGB pseudo-color
                                 images with depth information using the Taylor Series Channel Mixer (TSCM). This method enhances
                                 robustness to varying lighting conditions.
                             (b) Dataset quality. The MSDD dataset, with 9,332 annotated images covering eight defect types, ensures
                                 high model performance. The dataset’s diverse coverage of gravity-cast metal part defects enables effective
                                 model training for various industrial applications.
                             (c) Scalability. The method has demonstrated adaptability across different model architectures, including
                                 anchor-free, anchor-based, and ViT-based models. These general object detection models exhibit superior
                                 performance on the MSDD dataset compared to traditional static lighting approaches, highlighting the
                                 method’s flexibility. This shows that the method is independent of the underlying model architecture, mak-
                                 ing it scalable and suitable for detecting defects on surfaces with complex geometries and under various
                                 lighting conditions. This flexibility allows it to be integrated across different industrial settings, broadening
                                 its application potential.

                                 Limitations.         While our method has shown strong performance, there are several limitations that should be
                                 noted:

                                 (a) SIIA method on reflective surfaces. One of the key challenges we encountered is that the SIIA (Strobe
                                     Imaging Illumination Adjustment) method struggles with highly reflective surfaces. The complex arrange-
                                     ment of light sources tends to produce clear reflections on smooth surfaces, which can interfere with defect

www.nature.com/scientificdata/                                                                                     www.nature.com/scientificdata


                                 detection by creating visual artifacts that obscure the defects themselves. This is a significant issue when
                                 applying the model to surfaces like polished metals or glass.
                             (b) Imbalanced data. Another limitation involves the issue of data imbalance in our dataset, which could im-
                                 pact the model’s ability to generalize across various defect types. This is particularly evident when certain
                                 defect categories are underrepresented, potentially leading to skewed results or lower detection rates for
                                 rarer defects.


                             Future research directions.         To improve the model and bring it closer to industrial application, several
                             future research directions are worth exploring:

                             (a) Addressing imbalanced data. Continued data collection is essential to address the issue of imbalanced
                                 data categories. Expanding the dataset to include more diverse samples will improve the model’s ability to
                                 handle underrepresented defect types, leading to more robust performance across a variety of industrial
                                 scenarios.
                             (b) Testing on non-metallic surfaces. While initial experiments have demonstrated that the method works well
                                 on non-metallic surfaces, such as wood, leather, and fabric, showing promising extensibility, further exper-
                                 imentation and validation are necessary to confirm these findings across a wider variety of non-metallic
                                 materials.
                             (c) Improving detection accuracy. Additional efforts are required to improve the model’s detection accuracy
                                 to meet the stringent standards demanded by industrial environments. This can be achieved by optimizing
                                 the network architecture or incorporating more diverse training data to refine the model’s performance.


                             Usage Notes
                             In actual production, the majority of parts are defect-free, yet it is common to erroneously classify qualified parts
                             as defective. Therefore, this dataset includes a certain number of background images without defects to help
                             the model learn the patterns of normal parts. When training the dataset using general object detection models,
                             Translate, Scale, Mosaic, Random-Affine, MixUp, Augment HSV, and Horizontal Flip have been verified as
                             effective data augmentation methods. Additionally, unlike general object detection tasks, the features of surface
                             defects are highly correlated with the features of their surrounding background. Therefore, the positive and neg-
                             ative sample labeling strategy has a significant impact on the performance of the detection model.
